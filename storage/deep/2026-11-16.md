## [Objectives as Policies: Vision-Language Model Cost Programs for Contact-Rich Manipulation](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18298v1)

**PDF:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.18298v1
**Status:** reviewed
**Pages:** 8/8
**Coverage:** full extractable PDF text

**Research question:** How can we integrate the semantic understanding of vision-language models with physics-based model-predictive control to achieve open-world manipulation skills, without any training data or the scenes known a priori?

**Claimed contribution:** The paper introduces Objectives as Policies (OaP), a framework connecting a pretrained vision-language model to a shared sampling-based model-predictive control (MPC) through an editable objective program that specifies a multi-stage task sequence with running and terminal costs.

**Method:** The method builds a digital twin via real-to-sim, uses a pretrained VLM to generate and self-review an objective program from a predefined cost library, and executes the program using an MPPI controller paired with a stage verifier while iteratively refining the program using simulation rollout feedback.

**Datasets/evaluation:** Four tabletop manipulation tasks evaluated in simulation and real-world setups: pick-and-place, cup reorientation, direct push, and push with tool.

**Baselines:** Language to Rewards, ReKep, CoRAL (adapted), OpenVLA-OFT, and pi_0.5.

**Reported findings:** In simulation, OaP achieves a 90% cumulative success rate with at most two feedback rounds and 97.5% with up to five rounds. Real-world experiments achieve an overall success rate of 80% across 40 trials with scene perturbations.

**Limitations/threats to validity:** The framework depends on the fixed residual library and the accuracy of scene reconstruction and contact dynamics, lacks explicit recovery from failures after a stage has completed, and assumes rigid objects.

**Reproducibility checklist:** The authors provide a project website with videos and examples at https://zihaolu001.github.io/oap-project/, and specify key hyperparameters such as N=1000 optimization rollouts, H=600 physics steps, and MPC update limits.

**Recommended follow-up experiments:** Future work includes performing explicit system identification of shape, texture, and geometry parameters using differentiable simulation and rendering, adding online real-to-sim and real-world execution to the VLM feedback loop, tuning MPC hyperparameters, and comparing against agentic frameworks.

## [ManiLadder: Benchmarking Robot Manipulation Through a Categorized and Multi-Difficulty Level Task Ladder](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16374v1)

**PDF:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.16374v1
**Status:** reviewed
**Pages:** 11/11
**Coverage:** full extractable PDF text

**Research question:** What level of manipulation difficulty can a robot learning policy reliably solve, and how can robotic manipulation progress be systematically measured along the axis of task difficulty?

**Claimed contribution:** The authors present ManiLadder, a large-scale simulation benchmark containing 112 carefully designed tasks stratified into 4 difficulty levels across rigid-body and deformable-object manipulation, single-arm and dual-arm settings, and gripper and dexterous-hand end-effectors.

**Method:** ManiLadder fixes the amount of training data and employs a unified flow-matching imitation learning reference policy under standardized protocols, using evaluation success rates as an empirical difficulty signal to iteratively refine and calibrate tasks.

**Datasets/evaluation:** The benchmark uses 112 simulation tasks built upon Genesis with 20 demonstrations per task (totaling 2,240 trajectories at 20 Hz with synchronized multi-view RGB observations), plus 8 selected real-world tasks evaluated using an X-Arm and Meta Quest 3 teleoperation.

**Baselines:** The evaluation compares a small flow-matching policy, the π0.5 large pretrained vision-language-action model, and the GPT6-Astra agentic script policy.

**Reported findings:** Average success rates decrease monotonically from Level 1 to Level 4 across methods, but performance varies significantly by task suite. Transfer across difficulty levels yields little benefit, and a single policy trained jointly on all 112 tasks performs much worse than single-task policies (8.6% versus 33.5% overall).

**Limitations/threats to validity:** Calibration remains subject to the number of evaluation trials and the specific policy employed, and multi-task learning at benchmark scale remains an open challenge with degraded performance compared to isolated task training.

**Reproducibility checklist:** The authors assess reproducibility using three random seeds for demonstration recollection and retraining, yielding high task-level rank correlation (0.958), small success-rate changes (5.67 percentage points), and 96.4% level agreement.

**Recommended follow-up experiments:** Future work could investigate scalable multi-task architectures that prevent the performance degradation observed when jointly training on all 112 tasks, and expand the real-world validation set to cover higher difficulty levels.
